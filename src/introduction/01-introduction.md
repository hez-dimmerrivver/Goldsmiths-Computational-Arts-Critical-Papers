## Introduction

We have reached a critical moment where we now face challenges that are difficult to resolve, despite achieving technological advancements more than ever. Humanity has increased its dependence on technology by launching some 26,000 satellites into space and laying vast networks of fiber-optic cables underground on a global scale.<br>
This age of the machine makes everything seem automated. The machines can process, predict, and generate. However, we need to understand it cannot close the loop on its own. That final step, the act of seeing with intention, and of asking critically what the machine is doing behind, still requires a human to step into the feedback loop.<br>
This is the paradox that defines our moment. The more capable our machines become, the more urgently we are forced to ask what is distinctly human. Automation does not dissolve the question of humanity, it sharpens it. When a model can write, paint, and compose, we find ourselves asking what it means to write with a body, to paint from grief, to compose inside a dying ecosystem.<br>
A computational art practice sits within this shifting climate of evolving technology and varying levels of opacity. Research and writing becomes part of a creative practice that investigates the tension and dimensions of existing within a technoenvironment, as well as working with computation, information, and data as artistic material and process. <br>
The works gathered in this journal emerge from the authors’ own individual practices as MFA and MA students of Computational Arts at Goldsmiths, University of London. They explore what is quietly hollowed out beneath this current moment, the remainders that refuse to be tagged, tracked, averaged, or predicted.<br>
They share no common brief, yet the same preoccupation surfaces again and again: a suspicion of what computation claims it can capture, and an attention to what escapes. Together they move across three territories: Bodies & Embodiment, AI & Machine Vision, Ecology & Society. The first volume f0rm1ess, we do not rely on predefined forms to express our ideas. Instead, we explore the potential that exists between digits 0 and 1. 
We want to push beyond the binary idea that machines are either good or bad, and explore what it looks like to collaborate with them in an almost symbiotic relationship. We do not try to locate a finite point, which can be defined as “0.5” but rather constantly shift, derive, or exist as multiples at the same time. Instead, we ask: can all ecologies ranging from DNA to computer code rather exist as abstract poles of the machinic and the organic;; as spaces where the “other than”… the “else than”… the “post-”… the “more than” circulate?
