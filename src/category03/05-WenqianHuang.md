##### Wenqian Huang

# From Cloud to Cloud

## The Flâneur, Reflective Architecture, and the Circulation of Urban Images

<div class="body-text">

Glass in the city sometimes makes me stop.

A walk that begins with a practical purpose may be interrupted by a building surface. On a glass facade, the sky, streets, vehicles, pedestrians and surrounding buildings may appear at the same time. Things that belong to different distances and spatial layers are compressed by reflection into a temporary flat image. This image is never completely stable. It changes with light, weather, viewing angle and the position of the passer-by. When I move forward or step back, the reflection moves with me.

The glass facade is therefore not only an exterior material of architecture. It also participates in organising how the city is seen. A cloud in the sky becomes an optical image on the surface of a building. It may then be seen by the eye, captured by a smartphone, displayed on a screen, encoded as a file and stored in the cloud. The reflection does not end on the glass surface. It creates a space that is physical, optical, digital, networked and cloud-based.

Walter Benjamin’s The Arcades Project offers a way to understand this kind of urban attention. Benjamin writes about the arcades of nineteenth-century Paris, where glass, iron, shop windows, commodities, crowds and artificial lighting together formed a modern space between the street and the interior. The arcade was not only a passage for bodies. It was also a space in which looking became part of modern urban experience.

The flâneur, or urban wanderer, belongs to this environment. The flâneur is often understood as someone who wanders through the city, but wandering is not simply walking without direction. It is a way of allowing things encountered on the way to redirect attention. A window, a surface, a reflection, or a small visual detail may interrupt the original route and open another way of reading the city.

In the contemporary city, glass still produces this kind of interruption, but its condition has changed. In the arcade, glass framed commodities and mediated the relation between the street and the interior. In contemporary glass facades, the surface often hides the inside of the building and returns the outside world to the viewer. One does not see what happens inside the building, but instead sees clouds, roads, vehicles, neighbouring buildings and one’s own body spread across the facade.

The reflective facade can be understood as another kind of passage. It does not necessarily allow the body to pass through, but it allows light, space and image to move between different states. It is not simply a wall, nor only a mirror. It is a surface on which part of the city begins to take the form of an image.

Architecture comes before the camera.

The glass facade does not neutrally copy the outside world. It cuts the sky, compresses distance, alters brightness, and places distant buildings, moving clouds and the passer’s body onto the same surface. The person who later takes the photograph does not face an untouched reality, but an image already organised by architecture, light and the city.

This also explains why reflective architecture can invite photography. A cloud in the sky is open, without a frame, and does not easily form a stable composition with the human body. The facade gives it scale, division and position. Architecture has already done part of the image-making work: it turns a continuous and changing reality into an image that seems ready to be captured.

The smartphone does not preserve this image as it is. It matters here not only because it is a camera, but because it is an object carried with the body. It records, displays, stores and connects. A traditional camera can capture an image and keep it inside the device; a smartphone immediately displays the image on another surface and directs it towards files, accounts, platforms and cloud storage.

When the reflection is captured, light enters the sensor and becomes electronic signal. The device processes exposure, colour, contrast and dynamic range. The image may be brightened, sharpened, compressed and encoded. It may also be attached to time, location and device information. What appears on the screen may look like a record of what has just been seen, but it is already another version.

Architecture works with light and space; the smartphone works with signals and data. From glass to smartphone, the image shifts from an optical image to a computational image. The computational dimension is located in this transformation: the reflection is captured by a sensor, processed by software, displayed on a screen, encoded as a file, given metadata, and therefore made available for storage, search and circulation.

Here, the role of the flâneur also begins to change. At first, it is the human body that wanders. I move through the city, stop in front of the building and follow the reflection with my eyes. But once the image is captured, it is no longer only the body that moves. The image also begins to move.

It moves from the sky to the building surface, from the building surface to the eye, from the eye to the smartphone, from the sensor to the screen, from the screen to the file, and from the file to cloud storage or networked platforms. The flâneur begins as a person moving through the city, but the image becomes another kind of flâneur. It moves from one surface to another, from one space to another, from one cloud to another.

The first cloud is a meteorological event in the sky. It is formed by water vapour, temperature and air movement. It changes continuously and moves with the weather. It can only be seen from a particular place and at a particular moment.

The second cloud is the cloud of computational systems. It is not weather, but a structure of storage and access made of servers, data centres, cables, accounts, synchronisation systems and interfaces. It is called a “cloud”, as if it were light and immaterial, but it depends on concrete material infrastructures.

Between these two clouds, the image goes through a chain of transformations. The cloud in the sky becomes an optical reflection on glass. The reflection is seen by the human eye. It is captured by the smartphone sensor. It appears on the screen. It is encoded as a file. It enters cloud storage and may continue to circulate between platforms.

The first cloud reaches architecture as light. The second cloud receives it as data.

This is not a movement from reality into something unreal. The image passes through different material and technical conditions: atmosphere, facade, eye, sensor, screen, file, interface and cloud. Bratton’s account of layered computation can be useful here, because it helps to explain that cloud, city, interface, address and user are not separate worlds. Once the reflected image is captured by the smartphone, it enters a wider environment in which urban space and computational space overlap.

The smartphone also increases the number of surfaces on which the image can appear. The building surface carries one image; the eye receives another; the smartphone screen displays another; the file stores another; the cloud allows another version to be recalled later. These are not identical copies, but different states of the same visual event.

The physical cloud continues to move, and the photograph may seem to fix a particular moment. Yet the more contemporary issue is that the image does not truly stop. It continues to echo, repeat and circulate within computational systems. It can be synchronised, searched, forwarded, edited, archived and reappear on another screen.

The human flâneur remains in the physical city. The image continues elsewhere.

From cloud to cloud is therefore not a simple route of preservation. It is a media chain in which the image changes its state, location and conditions of visibility. What continues to exist in the second cloud is not a copy of the first cloud. It is another version, produced together by atmosphere, architecture, perception, smartphone camera, software and storage systems.

In this version, a contemporary way of seeing the city becomes clearer. People still walk through the street, but images have already moved beyond the street. The body may stop looking, but the image continues to wander.
