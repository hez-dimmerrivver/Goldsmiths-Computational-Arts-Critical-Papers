##### Jung Namkoong

# When Recognition Meets Re-cognition

## Visual Knowledge, Machine Vision, and the Epistemics of Recursive Reconstruction

<div class="body-text">

Are seeing and knowing the same thing? The assumption that they are runs deep in Western thought. Since Descartes proposed that the mind mirrors an independent reality, and Locke imagined ideas as copies of external things, philosophy has treated knowledge as a question of correspondence — how accurately the inner representation matches the outer world. Richard Rorty's critique of this tradition argues that the mirror metaphor is not a discovery about the nature of mind but a historically contingent habit mistaken for necessity: to verify that the mirror is accurate, one would need to step outside it and see the world directly, which is precisely what cannot be done (Rorty 1979). This article begins from that premise. It argues that neither human recognition nor machine vision functions as a mirror of reality, and that the assumption that either could — what this article calls the mirror paradigm — naturalises the specific, partial, and constructed character of both. When two different kinds of seeing inhabit the same space and claim authority over the same object, the transparency of reality becomes difficult to sustain. The question is not which vision is more accurate. It is what the mirror paradigm makes appear natural about each.
The first thing it naturalises is that both human and machine vision are forms of embodiment — different in kind, but equivalent in their partiality. Consider what the human visual system actually is: an eye sensitive to a specific range of wavelengths, connected to a brain that processes incoming signals through structures shaped by evolutionary history, cultural formation, and accumulated personal experience. Human vision does not see everything. It sees what it has been designed — by evolution, by culture, by lived experience — to see. Merleau-Ponty's account of embodied perception makes this precise: recognition does not happen only in the head but through the whole body (Merleau-Ponty 1962). Before any conscious calculation begins, the body already knows its place in the world — its orientation, its distances, its relations. This is what Merleau-Ponty calls the pre-reflective corporeal schema. To recognise a face — particularly one's own — is not to match a visual input against a stored template. It is to encounter the visible surface of a life that has been lived through a particular body. Inside that act of recognition are all the previous encounters, the feelings those encounters left behind, and the gradual revisions that experience has made to the image one holds of oneself.
Machine vision is equally embodied, though differently so. Its body is composed of training data, hardware architecture, algorithms, and thresholds — each of which functions in specific, designed ways that are particular to it, just as the eye and brain function in specific, evolved ways that are particular to them. The training data carries cultural weight and historical decisions about whose faces count as normative. The hardware imposes physical constraints on what can be processed and at what resolution. The algorithms encode a point of view, prioritising certain patterns over others. The thresholds enact value judgements about what counts as a valid result. What the machine sees is what it has been taught to see — and in this respect, the parallel with human vision is structural: we also see what we have been designed to see, by the accumulated selections of evolution and experience. The machine is not looking from nowhere. It is looking from a very specific somewhere: a training distribution, a set of design decisions, a history of inclusions and exclusions that shaped what it learned to recognise (Crawford and Paglen 2019).
The abstraction, then, is not the machine. It is the mirror paradigm — the mentality built into the presentation of machine vision as objective detection, as though the output were the world disclosing itself rather than the product of a particular, constructed way of seeing. This is what Haraway identifies as the god trick: the fantasy of a gaze that sees everything from no particular position (Haraway 1991). But a gaze requires eyes, eyes require a body, and a body is always somewhere. The god trick does not reside in the machine itself; it resides in the claim that the machine's output is neutral, in the institutional and cultural decision to present machine classification as a transparent window onto reality. Human vision operates within the same paradigm in its own way: it presents itself as simply natural, as just what seeing is, allowing the evolutionary, cultural, and affective conditions that shape it to recede into the background as though they were no conditions at all. Both visions are in the same relation to the mirror paradigm — the paradigm that naturalises each, making each appear as simply what seeing is rather than as a particular, constructed way of engaging with the world.

This is why the questions that matter are not questions about accuracy. They are the questions the mirror paradigm renders unremarkable: who built the training data? Which faces were included, and which were not? Whose features were encoded as normative, and whose were made marginal or invisible? Crawford and Paglen's analysis of large-scale image datasets demonstrates that these are political decisions, not technical ones — choices that encode assumptions about race, gender, and social value that are reproduced at scale in every act of classification the system subsequently performs (Crawford and Paglen 2019). When the mirror paradigm is set aside, what becomes visible is not a void but the machinery behind the result: the datasets, the design choices, the thresholds, and the consequences of error. The database is not a flawed copy of the world waiting to be improved. It is itself a way of seeing the world — one that determines in advance what is worth knowing, what counts as a face, and whose features are legible as normal (Sluis 2019).
If both human and machine vision are partial and embodied, what distinguishes them? The answer lies not in the presence or absence of a body but in how each relates to time and to the act of recognition itself. Human recognition is what this article terms re-cognition: the act of knowing again, shaped by the living accumulation of everything that has come before. Bergson's account of memory as duration rather than storage captures this precisely (Bergson 1911). Memory is not a filing system from which fixed records are retrieved; it is a living process that continues to move in the present, colouring each new act of perception. This means that the same face encountered twice is not encountered in quite the same way. Between the first meeting and the next, time passes, experience accumulates, and what one knows of that person shifts. Each act of recognition carries that accumulated weight into the present moment, and in doing so, modifies what will be recognised next time.
This is also why there is no such thing as a first sight in the strict sense. Even when a face is encountered for the first time, it is encountered through an already-formed perceptual system. Clark's predictive processing framework describes a brain that continuously generates expectations and reads incoming sensation as confirmation or correction of those expectations (Clark 2013). A new face is not processed from scratch. It is processed against the accumulated pattern of every face seen before, every cultural norm about what faces look like, every affective association the category of faces already carries. The entirely fresh perception is a philosophical fiction. Re-cognition precedes recognition.
Machine classification relates to time differently. In a stable system, the same input produces the same output. The machine does not accumulate experience of a particular face across encounters; it does not revise its understanding of that face in light of what has happened since. Where human re-cognition is perpetually in motion — modifying itself through every new encounter — machine classification is fixed at the moment of training until the system is retrained. Even post-training feedback loops, the cybernetic corrections introduced through user interaction, constitute a separate learning process rather than a modification of the recognition act itself. The machine recognises, in a sense, only once: it applies a trained model to a new input without the act of application changing the model in the moment.
This asymmetry matters because it defines what each system can and cannot carry. Human re-cognition carries history — the specific, irreplaceable deposit of every encounter, every emotion, every revision. It carries flesh, in Merleau-Ponty's sense: the experience of being a body that is also seen, that meets the world as both subject and object (Merleau-Ponty 1962). Machine classification carries data — the curated, annotated, politically inflected version of the world that was encoded in its training. Both are partial. Both are shaped by what came before. But they are shaped by different befores, and they revise themselves — or fail to revise themselves — in different ways.

The mirror paradigm naturalises this difference away. By presenting both as kinds of seeing that aspire toward the same end — an accurate reflection of an independent reality — it allows human vision to appear simply natural and machine vision to appear simply objective. The specific conditions on which each depends recede into the background. Setting the mirror aside does not leave a void. It leaves two genuinely different ways of knowing — each with its own body, its own history, its own partiality — and the political question of what happens when one of them is presented as though its specific conditions were no conditions at all: as though it were simply detecting the world, rather than seeing it from a very particular somewhere.
