# AI’s Media Mix

## AI’s Dangerous Affinity With Anime

<div class="body-text">

The year is 2025, three years since Stable Diffusion hit the market. A colleague and I were walking through the subway platform of Shanghai when a peculiar-looking poster entered our vicinity. The advert featured an anime-styled illustration of two people. The girl in front looked like she’d been meticulously rendered by an artist, whereas the boy in the background had smeared, half-fused fingers. The generic yet uncanny depiction immediately presented itself as "AI art". My colleague, who happened to be invested in this field, made snarky comments that the image was a product of an outdated "checkpoint model", and that he would have used "state-of-the-art" illustration models such as _Illustrious-XL_ and _NoobAI-XL_. I jokingly asked him if he could reverse-engineer the prompt used to produce the image in front of us, and watched a long chain of AI runes eject from his mouth.

`1girl, 1boy, looking at viewer, hands up, short hair, dark skin, school uniform, best quality, masterpiece.`

**AI and Danbooru**

Both _Illustrious-XL_ and _NoobAI-XL_ have been trained on short, discrete tags as uttered by my colleague, and both point to the website "Danbooru" as the origin of this style of tagging. Launched in 2005, Danbooru is an image-hosting website intended to be a "high-quality archive for anime-styled images". The archive is co-curated by its users, as they submit images and are encouraged to contribute tags that describe the image. The large number of posts (11 million as of now), coupled with the refined pairing between images and tags, made Danbooru the ideal repository to train image models on. Its first interaction with AI happened in 2017 as writer Gwern scraped its data for classification research, producing the dataset `danbooru2017` (Gwern 2015). In 2022, company NovelAI trained its proprietary text-to-image model using the site’s data, which was subsequently leaked in a security breach (NovelAI 2022). The leaked model, now known as `animefull-latest.ckpt`, showed the Internet that "AI can draw anime", thus spawning a gold rush at Danbooru where users bulk-scraped its data for model fine-tuning. The site’s traffic skyrocketed alongside its significance — its vocabulary of tags would soon become a standard prompting format adopted by many models, and has gained far more popularity than the natural-language captioning format associated with OpenAI's CLIP (Wagner and Cetinic 2025).

Looking at the stunning output of the aforementioned models, it becomes hard to dismiss Danbooru’s feat in the field of generative AI, whether that be its original intention or not. Moreover, Danbooru’s method of data curation stands in stark contrast to prior datasets such as ImageNet. While the latter outsourced the labour of labelling to data workers at Amazon Mechanical Turk, Danbooru relied completely on the volunteered co-curation of its user community, who collectively decided the tagging conventions. This is augmented by the site’s open-source codebase, which has allowed numerous "image-boorus" to emerge, each serving its own fandom (Britt 2019). One could argue that Danbooru represents an alternative approach to the making of an AI system — one that is federated, community-driven and non-profit, standing against the dominant players in the industry.

However, before one envisions a bright future of AI based upon Danbooru, the present criticisms of the site seem more glaring. The most heated debates are on the issue of "art theft", as the majority of Danbooru’s images are uploaded without the consent of their original artists. The taxonomy, on closer inspection, is also itself skewed, containing an expansive list of sexually explicit terms while remaining thin on domains such as nature or art history (Britt 2019). There is also `asian` (but not `european`), `bad vulva` (but not `bad penis`), a symptom of the far-right and gender-exclusionary pre-discourses that haunt the anime fandom (Petit 2022). Researchers have also traced how Danbooru-derived tagging systems normalised sexualised and misogynistic depictions in downstream generative pipelines, fostering an ecosystem where fantasy is misapplied to real-life individuals through deepfake models (Wagner and Cetinic 2025). What struck me in the subway, however, was that the site has furnished a system in which an artwork can be flattened into an array of tags, and the suggestion that this reduction can be run in reverse to produce endless combinations of art in the fashion of Fordist manufacture.

**Media Mix, Worlds and Databases**

Or perhaps that wasn’t Danbooru’s fault, but an established mode of consumption and production in the anime-manga industry. Culture critic Azuma Hiroki coined the term "database consumption" in his analysis of otaku culture in the 1990s. He proposed that each piece of character art, upon being received, is broken down into a collection of "_Moe_-elements" drawn from a "database". "_Moe_-elements" are parts of a character design that evoke an affectionate response from its audience, which may include "`cat ears`", "`short hair`", "`maid uniform`", or "`glasses`" — exactly what Danbooru's tags are about. On the other end, the industry has adopted a method of media production based on the database — "create character settings first, followed by the work or project, including stories" (Azuma 2001).

This method of media production is part of a broader phenomenon known as "Media Mix", a term often associated with the Japanese publishing giant _Kadokawa_. "Media Mix" originally denoted the practice of serial franchising of media across different platforms in post-war Japan (Steinberg 2023), though it has gained further complexity since Kadokawa Tsuguhiko took over the company in 1993. Inspired by the then-popular TRPGs (table-top role-playing games) such as Dungeons & Dragons, Tsuguhiko’s approach to storytelling involved designing a "world view" — a set of rules and character blueprints, which could then be used to enact stories through the distribution of creative agency (Otsuka 2004). This version of Media Mix is interested not in producing narratives but in building a mechanism that can generate an infinite number of narratives through the recombination of narrative elements. Characters are one of these elements, which Azuma sees as the "meta-narrative nodal point" that holds narratives together. "The know-how for enhancing the attractiveness (through the art of the Moe-element) of characters has rapidly accumulated… and the development of Moe-element databases has become a necessity" (Azuma 2001). Azuma’s insistence on the term "database" reveals the computational nature of the practice of Media Mix, and its similarity with "Generative AI" as we know it today. _AI Dungeon_, an early face of GPT-2, realised Tsuguhiko's TRPG-derived vision of storytelling almost exactly. Diffusion models, meanwhile, generate character art from tag-annotated datasets that are offsprings of Danbooru — the manifestation of the "Moe-element database" as it was conceptualised by Azuma.

**The Empowerment Deceit**

Another characteristic of the Media Mix is the mobilisation of consumers as producers. One should be familiar with the culture of "doujin" where fan-made derivative works circulate at conventions as well as online. The creativity amongst the fans is celebrated by Ian Condry in _The Soul of Anime_ (2013), and resonates with Western studies of fandom such as the works on participatory culture by Henry Jenkins. Another who credited fan creativity is Otsuka Eiji, who worked for Kadokawa as one of the early practitioners of "Media Mix" (and later, a harsh critic of it). In his text _World and Variation_ (1989), Otsuka saw the model of media production they had developed as a system that could empower the consumer of texts. Since Media Mix delivers a "world view", consumers who internalised the "world" are able to produce small narratives of their own using its building blocks (such as characters). This would end in the dissolution of the boundary between the "official" and the "derivative" (Otsuka 1989). A similar kind of empowerment discourse exists amongst the field of generative AI, which suggests the technology could empower the ordinary user by eliminating the skill required to create, as prompting replaces the artistic training required to produce art. Though, as Marc Steinberg points out in his analysis of Media Mix and its platform economy (Steinberg 2019), such production is being actively harvested as a resource. Kadokawa in particular published collections of fan-made derivative works of franchises it owns, and launched the web novel platform _Kakuyomu_ in 2016, where the company’s editors scout the site’s metrics for amateur web novels valuable for publishing and adapting. Users not only contribute content to the site but also participate in the curation of content through their views and ratings. The generative AI industry replicates this arrangement at scale. An example would be aforementioned NovelAI which trained its proprietary model on Danbooru's fan-tagged database, whose images are in turn sourced from fan illustration sites such as _Pixiv_. Such is the fate of the participatory culture media mix cultivated: consumers are empowered just to the extent that they can be extracted. A seemingly democratic platform in fact institutionalises a system where users absorb the precarity of uncompensated labour.

The deception of empowerment does not stop at an economic level. In his later work, Otsuka traced Media Mix and its participatory model back to wartime propaganda employed by the Imperial Rule Assistance Association of Japan. The very techniques of distributing character and a "world view" for the consumers to elaborate on were pioneered in _Yokusan Ikka_ (1940), a state-sanctioned manga project whose cast of characters were designed to be easy to draw and was deliberately released for amateurs and artists alike to produce their own stories with. What’s embedded within the characters and their "world view", however, is a fascist ideology that justified Japan's colonisation of the East Asia. Here, the participatory culture was turned towards mass mobilisation (Otsuka 2018). Through their fannish creation of small narratives, citizens voluntarily internalised the state's ideology all whilst perpetuating it as their comics circulated via mass media. The consumer’s creative agency within a system like Media Mix is bounded by its construct, as the "world view" (or "database") handed to the amateur artist predetermines what can be said within it, much like the dataset of a generative AI model delimits what is possible in its output. Juggling the tags of the text-to-image community yields illustrations that meet its codified standard of anatomical correctness and beauty — `4k`, `masterpiece`, `amazing` `quality`, `award-winning`. The user is free to recombine these graphical clichés and circulate the way of seeing they encode, but never to redefine them.

**Depature**

I hesitate to close the case. Media Mix, the same participatory framework that once mobilised citizens for wartime propaganda, also cultivated the self-publishing scene of doujin, where fans sustained decades of genuine innovation and no shortage of subversion. Danbooru, despite everything it has fed into, remains as a vast archive built and governed by its own community. Perhaps the question is not what made this collaborative culture complicit, but whether any collaborative framework can escape the capture. Could a database belong to its builders, before it belongs to who runs the mechanism on top of it?

The train arrived at the platform. My colleague was still refining his prompt as we boarded, chasing a better render of the girl. The advert would be seen by millions of commuters before its contract ended. No one drew the picture, yet everyone did. The doors closed and the two protagonists slid out of view, `looking at viewer`, until the end.
