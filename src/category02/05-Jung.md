##### Jung Namkoong

# When Recognition Meets Re-cognition

## Visual Knowledge, Machine Vision, and the Epistemics of Recursive Reconstruction

<div class="body-text">

We tend to treat seeing and knowing as the same thing. If we saw it with our own eyes, we assume it must be true. This seems so obvious that it rarely gets questioned. But the philosopher Richard Rorty argues that this equation — seeing equals knowing — is itself the problem. It is not a fact about how the world works. It is a habit of thought that Western philosophy has taken for granted for a long time. Since Descartes proposed that the mind accurately reflects the world like a mirror, we have understood knowledge as a question of how accurately that mirror works. Rorty's objection is simple: to check whether the mirror is accurate, you would need to step outside it and look at the world directly — but that is exactly what you cannot do. You are always looking through the mirror. This essay starts from that unsettling point. If no vision — human or machine — can be a perfect mirror, what happens when two very different kinds of seeing confront the same object and each claims to know it?

To answer this, we need to distinguish between two ways of seeing that are usually collapsed into one. The way humans recognise a face and the way a machine processes one are not simply more or less accurate versions of the same operation. They work according to entirely different principles. When a person recognises a face, what is happening is not just the processing of visual information. Memory and Feeling are involved. The accumulated history of every prior encounter with that person is involved. This essay calls that process _re-cognition_ — knowing again. Not seeing for the first time, but seeing through everything you have come to know. The philosopher Henri Bergson described memory not as a filing system that stores and retrieves records, but as something living that continues to move in the present. When you recognise someone's face, you are not opening a file and checking it against what is in front of you. You are letting the time you have shared with that person flow into the present moment. Merleau-Ponty added that this recognition does not happen only in the head — it happens through the whole body. To know your own face is not an abstract calculation. It is the act of a living being who exists in the world through a particular body.

Machine vision works entirely differently. A machine has no prior history with the face it processes, no feeling about it, no body. It breaks the image down into numerical values, compares them against learned patterns, and outputs a result. Crucially, what the machine has learned from is not the world itself but a curated version of it — images that were selected, labelled, and categorised by people, according to choices that were never neutral. Kate Crawford and Trevor Paglen have shown that these labelling decisions are always shaped by particular assumptions: about which faces count as normal, which features matter, whose appearance the system is designed to recognise well. The machine is not a mirror of reality. It is a structure built from someone else's decisions.

Does this mean human recognition is better and machine recognition is worse? This essay claims a different direction. The more interesting question lies elsewhere. We now live in a moment when both of these ways of seeing operate in the same space, making competing claims about the same objects. When a witness's testimony and a facial recognition system's result conflict in a courtroom, when a doctor's judgement and a diagnostic algorithm disagree, which one do we follow? There is no shared standard by which to settle the question, because the two visions are not better and worse versions of the same thing. They are different kinds of seeing altogether, built for different purposes, operating on different principles. This essay frames that as a question of _co-inhabitation_ rather than competition.

Trevor Paglen's work makes clear how deep this difference goes. The vision systems built into a self-driving car are not trying to see the road the way a human driver does. They were designed for something else entirely — to classify objects quickly and reliably, to detect pedestrians, to read traffic lights. They do not see the beauty of a street or the anxiety of a pedestrian. That is not a failure. It is what they were made to do. Paglen describes these systems as having their own _character_ — a point of view organised around a specific function. And this is where Rorty's critique becomes directly relevant. If no vision is a neutral mirror of the world, where does the machine's claim to objectivity come from? Not from the machine actually being objective. It comes from the fact that the machine has inherited, without knowing it, the old philosophical assumption that accurate detection equals accurate reflection of reality. The output presents itself as the world disclosing itself directly — but it is, in fact, the product of training data and design choices that remain invisible in the result.

When these two visions look at the same object and produce different accounts of it, there is no neutral position from which to judge which one is right. Donna Haraway calls the fantasy of such a position the _god trick_ — the idea of a gaze that sees everything from nowhere in particular. But a gaze requires eyes, eyes require a body, and a body is always somewhere. There is no vision without a location. Neither the machine's detection result nor the human's recognition can step outside its own conditions to serve as the final arbiter. This is not a nihilistic conclusion — it does not mean that nothing can be known or that all views are equally valid. It means that when we abandon the mirror model, what becomes visible is not a void but the machinery behind the result: who built the training data, which faces were included and which were not, who bears the consequences when the system gets it wrong. These are questions that the mirror model kept hidden.

This is where re-cognition matters. If the mirror was never a coherent model of knowing — for human or machine — then recognition cannot mean the accurate replay of a stored original. Re-cognition never claimed to do that. It is the act of constructing something again, differently each time, through the weight of what has come before. This departs from an assumption that runs through much of machine learning: that recognition and generation are equivalent, that to recognise a face is simply to reproduce it accurately. Re-cognition says something else. Knowing a face was always a partial, situated reconstruction — closer in structure to what machines do than we usually admit. But where machine classification is shaped entirely by the data it was trained on, re-cognition is grounded in memory, body, and lived experience. That difference — not a difference of accuracy, but a difference in what the knowing is made of and where it comes from — is what this essay sets out to explore.
