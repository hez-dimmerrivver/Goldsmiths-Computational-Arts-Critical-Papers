# Bypassing

## Yiran Jiao

**An Empty Judgment Produced by Technology**

<div class="body-text">

Imagine that one day, you are told that an algorithmic risk system has placed you on a watchlist. Your past records, your place of residence, your social relations: this set of quantifiable features is close enough to those of a certain high-risk group; and so, even though you may not have committed any illegal act, you still receive a determination about yourself: a score, a risk level, contact from the police. This system, Chicago's "Strategic Subject List," ran for several years before it was shut down.¹ So what I want to ask is: why can an operational result about an individual be produced before that individual's concrete action has even taken place, and change the way they are treated?

¹ The "Strategic Subject List" is taken here as an example because it is a closed case that has already run its full course and can be looked back on; but this logic did not end when it was shut down. Similar predictive tracking, targeting, and risk-determination systems are still widely deployed today across different contexts. What this essay is concerned with is their shared structure, not the specific circumstances of any single deployment.

## **1\. Judgment**

To see clearly what this system does to judgment, we must first describe the old structure of judgment. Following Mireille Hildebrandt's account of the "informational" character of law[^1], modern legal judgment is organized around actions that have already taken place, not around some persistent state of the subject. Concrete action is the object of judgment: a person can anticipate in advance that signing a contract and then failing to perform it may bring liability, precisely because the connection between "action and consequence" lets the meaning of a behaviour be grasped before it unfolds and confirmed after it unfolds. Reasons are the mediation through which an action can be interpreted; a transfer of money may be fraud, may be support of a dependent, may be repayment of a debt, and the same external behaviour points to different structures of responsibility under different reasons. Context is the background through which action and reasons can be read. These three together support the whole structure.

At the same time, Hildebrandt adds a feature that is crucial to what follows: judgment can be modified. It is not a result that forms once and is thereby closed, but a process continually reopened by new actions, facts, and explanations. [^2]The subject's later behaviour flows back, and can loosen the initial handling. Even an everyday prejudgment based on accent or clothing is no more neutral than an algorithmic score; but it takes place within a concrete scene, and a person can resist it with action. This capacity to be reopened is precisely what keeps judgment open to the correction of itself.

## **2\. "Judgment"**

Now return to the "Strategic Subject List." Its operational logic is to infer future risk from past data. A person's records, location, risk of victimisation, and social relations are entered into the model, in order to calculate the possibility that they will become a shooting victim or perpetrator in the future. The person placed on the list may never have carried out any violent act; they may not even have done anything in the present scene that needs to be examined. They enter the system only because their data position is close enough to that of a certain high-risk group. What Antoinette Rouvroy and Thomas Berns call the "profile" comes into play here: what the system makes contact with is a statistical position recomposed from digital traces and everyday trajectories.[^3] These materials strictly belong to the past, and often do not belong to the subject's own actions: a high score may originate from the neighbourhood they lived in a few years ago, or from several records of co-appearing with a marked person. It is thin in attribution. What it records is a network position. What the subject did as an agent barely enters the calculation. And so the three conditions are absent one by one:

**Concrete action disappears:** the basis for handling this person is a calculated closeness to future risk, and has nothing to do with what they did.

**Reasons disappear**: what the model gives is correlation, a relation of co-occurrence between data points, and it cannot be refuted or corrected the way an interpretation of reasons can.

**Context disappears, or rather is displaced**: the background is past data compressed into features, and it often belongs to a group: the crime rate of a neighbourhood, the statistical profile of a category of population, while the consequences fall on the individual.

And at the same time, that capacity to "be reopened" is also absorbed in reverse. If a person marked as high-risk later carries out no violence, this does not overturn the handling of them. It can be read as the risk having never existed, or as the risk having already been preemptively intervened in, and either way, the score is preserved. The subject's later behaviour, their appeals, and even the fact that "the predicted behaviour never happened" are all absorbed as inputs into the model's next round of self-optimisation. What once could reopen judgment is converted into material that makes the system more precise and therefore more impossible to hold accountable. The channel of correction becomes a device that reinforces the initial handling.

This is the structure I want to call "empty judgment. "Recent work on predictive justice describes the same phenomenon. Quattrocolo (2026) discusses a case in which a traffic stop was initiated solely because it took place in an area that a predictive model had marked in advance as "high-risk." [^4]The court noted that this mode of enforcement had shifted policing away from judgment grounded in individualized suspicion, toward a preemptive logic rooted in prediction rather than concrete suspicion. Judgment is still operating, complete and efficient, with classification, contact, intervention, and consequences all falling on the subject. But the action that was its object, the reasons that were its mediation, the context that let it be read, and the openness that kept it modifiable no longer pass through the subject, no longer need the subject's building and repair; in their place, an external eye examines and measures the distance between the subject and each of the standards.

So technology opens a wormhole at the very place where judgment used to unfold. The stretch of process that should have been held up by action, that should have taken shape slowly in time and in waiting, is bypassed whole, hollowed out, and pressed flat. At the same time, whenever this wormhole meets a person's appeals against it, it rejects them completely, and goes on pulling those appeals in, turning them into material that makes it more solid, ignoring every effort that tries to change it. And what do all these bypassed things stand for, these actions, reasons, and contexts?—I take them to be precisely the conditions that depend on a span of time that has not yet arrived, that depend on an event still waiting to unfold. They cannot be computed in advance, because they have not yet happened.

And these bypassed conditions are precisely the qualification by which a person is treated as a subject who can argue back, can respond, can be understood anew. When judgment can take effect as usual while bypassing them, what is suspended is the subject's position to speak within this judgment. This, of course, we already know. What I want to raise is a further question beyond it. "Judgment" has always been taken as a single whole, its conditions and its outcome hard to pull apart. But if a "predictive system" became accurate enough, accurate enough to absorb an individual's appeals and, on that basis, actually change the outcome it gives, handing down an adjusted "perfect judgment," then has this synthetic empty judgment already come to possess the reality of "judgment"? And the sense of "being judged" that a person receives within it, what kind of synthetic artefact is that?

One line of research argues that subjects determined by an algorithm should regain channels to contest, to respond, and to demand explanations, and that once these channels are designed into the system, the subject's position to respond can be restored (Yurrita et al., 2025\).[^5] But when a system becomes accurate enough to absorb these contestations and to adjust its own outcomes accordingly, I think the situation only grows worse. The more a system can respond to appeals, the more thoroughly it hollows out: each "perfect" adjustment produces a new judgment that has bypassed the constitutive conditions, and so produces more and more of an undefinable, incomplete "experience of being judged."
