##### Yiran Jiao

# Bypassing

## An Empty Judgment Produced by Technology

<div class="body-text">

Imagine that one day, you are told that an algorithmic risk system has placed you on a watchlist. Your past records, your place of residence, your social relations: this set of quantifiable features sits close enough to the features of some high-risk group. As a result, even though you may not have committed any unlawful act, you still receive a determination about yourself: a score, a risk level, a contact from the police. This system is Chicago's "Strategic Subject List", a system that ran for several years before being shut down (Saunders, Hunt, and Hollywood 2016).¹ So the question I want to ask is: why can a judgment directed at an individual appear before that individual's concrete action has taken place?

¹ The "Strategic Subject List" is used as the example here because it is a case that has already ended and can be reviewed in full. After the system was shut down, its logic did not end with it; this essay is concerned with the structure they share.

**1\. Judgment**

To see clearly what this system does to judgment, we must first describe the old structure of judgment. According to Mireille Hildebrandt's account of the "informational" character of law: modern legal judgment is organised around actions that have already taken place (Hildebrandt 2016);

The concrete action is the object of judgment: a person can anticipate in advance that failing to perform a contract after signing it may bring liability, precisely because the connection between action and consequence allows the meaning of a behaviour to be understood before it unfolds,

Reasons are the medium through which an action can be interpreted; a transfer of money may be fraud, or it may be support for a dependant. The same external behaviour, under different reasons, points to different structures of responsibility.

Context is the background against which actions and reasons can be read. Together, these three hold up the entire structure.

At the same time, Hildebrandt adds a feature that is crucial for what follows: judgment can be modified. Rather than a result that closes the moment it is formed, judgment is a process continually reopened by new actions, facts, and interpretations (Hildebrandt 2015). The subject's later conduct flows back and may loosen the initial treatment. It is this capacity to be reopened that keeps judgment open to self-correction.

**2\. "Judgment"**

So what changes in judgment once the system intervenes? Gallese discusses a case in which a traffic stop was initiated entirely because it took place in an area a predictive model had marked in advance as "high-risk" (Gallese 2026). The court pointed out that this mode of enforcement had shifted policing from judgment based on individualised suspicion towards a preventive logic rooted in prediction rather than concrete suspicion. So it is with the "Strategic Subject List". Its operating logic is the same as in the case above: inferring future risk from past data. A person placed on the list may never have committed any violent act; they may not even have done anything in the present scene that calls for scrutiny. They enter the system because of the data extracted from them. Here the "profiling" described by Antoinette Rouvroy and Thomas Berns comes into play: what the system touches is a statistical position recomposed from digital traces and everyday trajectories (Rouvroy and Berns 2013). Strictly speaking, these materials belong to the past, and often they do not belong to the subject's own actions: a high score may stem from records of their having appeared several times alongside someone already flagged. In attribution, it is thin. At this point the judgment is complete, yet the three conditions for completing a judgment are absent one by one:

**The concrete action disappears:** the basis for handling this person is a computed proximity to future risk, which has nothing to do with what they have done.

**Reasons disappear:** what the model delivers is correlation, the co-occurrence of data points, with no stage at which anyone, when asked, gives a reason.

**Context is displaced:** the background becomes past data compressed into features, and this data often belongs to a group, while the consequences fall on the individual.!

At the same time, that capacity "to be reopened" is absorbed in reverse. If a person marked as high-risk later commits no violence, this can be read as the risk never having existed, or as the risk having been preventively contained. Either way, the score is retained. The subject's later conduct, their appeals, even the fact that the predicted behaviour never occurred, are all absorbed as input for the model's next round of self-optimisation. This makes the system more precise and therefore harder to hold accountable; the channel of correction becomes a device that reinforces the initial treatment.

This is the structure I want to call the "empty judgment". Judgment still operates, complete and efficient: classification, contact, intervention, and consequences all land on the subject. Yet action, reasons, context, and the openness to modification no longer appear in the process of judgment. Technology opens a wormhole at the very place where judgment used to unfold. The stretch of process that should have been supported by action, that should have slowly taken shape in time and in waiting, is bypassed as a whole, hollowed out, flattened. What is hollowed out is the process itself, the road between action and verdict, the time of waiting; the subject stands intact at the exit of the wormhole and receives the result. Whenever an appeal is thrown at it, the wormhole draws it in and grows more solid. And what do these bypassed things stand for? What do these actions, reasons, and contexts stand for? — I take them to be precisely the conditions that depend on a stretch of time not yet arrived, on an event still waiting to unfold. They cannot be computed in advance, because they have not yet happened.

**3\. Separation**

And these bypassed conditions are exactly where a person's standing to be treated as a subject resides; when judgment can take effect as usual while bypassing these conditions, what is suspended is the subject's position to speak within that judgment. In recent years, one line of research has sought to respond to this suspension through design: channels for appeal, response, and demanding explanation are seen as key to protecting the dignity and autonomy of those being judged, and this line studies how to build such channels into systems according to their needs (Yurrita and others 2025). But in my view, this optimisation brings further disregard of autonomy. Even if the system becomes accurate enough to absorb an individual's appeal and, on that basis, genuinely change the result it gives, delivering an adjusted "perfect judgment".

Why? In the old structure, correction passes through reinterpretation: the subject's statement is read as a reason, placed back into its context, connected to an action, and the verdict changes because the understanding of a person has changed. But in the responsive system, the appeal enters as data, and the verdict changes only because the computation has been run once more. The two results can look identical. A person is flagged; they appeal; the score is lowered; they are removed from the list. Their own conduct did change the outcome, and the weight of the consequences landing on them is no different from that of a verdict that has passed through the full process; from the result alone, they can no longer tell whether they have been seen or recomputed. The more perfect a predictive system, the more open it is to being influenced by appeal, the more it satisfies the requirement of contestability on the surface, while the appeal is absorbed as data throughout. A perfect judgment is a judgment in which even modifiability has been synthesised. But return to what came before: the action supporting this treatment was never examined, reasons were never given, and the context never belonged to them. Judgment in the sense of the old process did not happen; what happened was the entirety of its effects. Then what is this thing that lands on them, and where does it come from? It is produced by computation, and its origin contains no event that has already taken place.

As far as existing systems go, this artefact is not yet perfect. Appeals are absorbed as data, scores are retained, and the subject still has a chance to notice, in the seams of the results, that what they face is a computation run once more. The real question arises in the perfect system imagined above: there, the experience the system delivers passes, at the level of effect, every test the subject is able to perform. This is something new: an artificial experience of "being judged", detached from the event that should have generated it, and holding nonetheless. I have no intention of disparaging it; it does act on the subject, and it does respond to the subject's conduct; what can be described is that it did not arrive along the old process.

What can be established ends here: the hollowing-out has already taken place, empty judgments are already being produced, and the closer the system comes to perfection, the harder this output is to distinguish from judgment. When that perfect artefact arrives, whether what the subject receives is a judgment or an experience of "being judged", and how that experience should be defined, this essay hands over as an open question.
