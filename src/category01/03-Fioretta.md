##### Keyi Zhang (Fioretta)

# Onycho Lab Artificial Nails as Bodily Technology

<div class="body-text">

How do we determine the boundaries of the human body? When are these boundaries violated?

This question began for me with a daily life experience: wearing long artificial nails. Every time I get nail extensions, my hands feel strange for at least two or three weeks. My fingers seem longer than before. The distance I can reach changes. My gestures become slower, more cautious, sometimes less functional. I type differently, pick up objects differently, and touch surfaces with a slightly altered awareness. When the nails are removed, my body must recalibrate again. My fingers feel shorter. The world seems to return to a previous distance. This small cosmetic modification produces a temporary illusion: my brain begins to treat an artificial extension as part of the body.

**Artificial Nails as Interactable Wearable Devices**

What it means to the body has been redefined by technologies that are increasingly absorbed into everyday embodiment. I was inspired by Andy Clark’s concept of mind extension (Clark 2003). We understand that tools and technologies act as continuous extensions of the human mind and body. However, artificial nails complicate this functionalist narrative. Clark argues that an external entity coupled to our body qualifies as an extension of our mind if it performs a function that is sufficiently similar to an internal cognitive process. When we rely on a notebook to store information that we would otherwise remember, the notebook becomes an extension of the mind because it occupies the same functional role as biological memory (Clark and Chalmers 1998).

I accept the spirit of Clark's thesis, but I argue that functional equivalence is not the only basis for extension. An external object may become part of the body or mind not by replicating an existing function but by reorganising the body's functional capacities in ways that the body comes to treat as its own. Artificial nails illustrate this alternative. Unlike the notebook, they do not simply enhance or imitate the functions of biological nails or fingers. Yet their significance lies precisely in their capacity to reorganise bodily action. As the wearer adapts, the brain recalibrates motor planning so that movements are executed as if the artificial nails were part of the hand itself. The body thus reorganises its functional architecture around the external object. What legitimises the artificial nails as an extension of the body is therefore not functional equivalence but functional integration through reorganisation.

To explore this idea, I created _Onycho Lab_: a performative computational installation that asks how a seemingly insignificant, decorative object can reconfigure the human body’s perception of its own boundaries.

After multiple prototyping, I developed a wearable haptic feedback system to symbolically simulate the sensory experience at the fingertips. Micro-pressure sensors are embedded at the distal tip of the artificial nail, acting as synthetic nerve endings. When the extension makes physical contact with a surface, the sensor triggers a tiny vibration motor adhered to the dorsal side, the back, of the biological finger. By displacing the site of impact from the tip of the prosthetic to the skin of the wearer, the system creates a closed sensation loop. The artificial nail, therefore, becomes an interactable wearable device.

**Between Salon and Clinic**

Beyond the wearable device, the work also recognises manicure as a body-modification procedure. Manicure is commonly understood as a beauty service, an act of care, or a commercial ritual associated with femininity and self-presentation (Kang 2010). However, if we look closely, the process of manicure shares a similar procedural structure with medical intervention. Before entering the space, one often makes an appointment, clarifies needs and expectations, discloses relevant bodily information, and prepares for a treatment. During the procedure, the hand is disinfected, examined, held still, and worked upon with specialised tools. Something may be removed from the body; something may be added. The body is modified through some specific expertise.

Of course, manicure and surgery are not identical. Their risks, legal status, institutional authority, and social meanings are radically different. _Onycho Lab_ stages manicure as a performative surgery in order to reveal the procedural and political structures that are already present within beauty work (Kang 2010). Both manicure and surgery involve intimate bodily access. Both require trust. Both depend on tools, hygiene, technical knowledge, and the temporary passivity of the person being touched. Both can be caring, but also be controlling. Both transform the body through a relation of expertise. The difference lies in the degree.

Given the above functional resemblance, the authority, power, and status of the doctor and the manicurist are still valued very differently. Think about it. Medical touch is protected by institutional power, professional status, and the language of necessity. Manicure touch is often feminised, commercialised, racialised, and dismissed as service labour (Kang 2010). Yet the body does not experience this labour as trivial. Some parts of the body must still be surrendered. The client must remain still. The practitioners will still touch the body and perform certain procedures. The apparent softness of beauty service can conceal a complex choreography of bodily compliance.

This observation motivated me to design an installation setting that sits between a salon, a clinic, and a laboratory. The project was created as a performative computational installation that reprograms the ordinary act of manicure into a body-modification procedure. Participants enter a staged environment that sits between a nail salon, a clinic, and a laboratory. They are invited to book or enter an appointment, read basic information, sign a consent form, and offer their hand for a procedure. I perform as a fictional haptic doctor: masked, gloved, emotionally restrained, and precise in gesture. The participant sits under intense light while their hand is held, positioned, fitted, and tested. What begins as a familiar beauty service gradually becomes displaced into something more clinical, more procedural, and more ambiguous.

_Onycho Lab_ brings the conflict among clinics and salons into the installation environment. Translucent white gauze surrounds the space, creating a semi-private and semi-public enclosure. From the outside, viewers can only vaguely glimpse the procedure inside, as if witnessing something intimate but not fully accessible. From within, participants temporarily detach from the ordinary exhibition space and enter a fictional clinical field. Strong spotlights illuminate the participant’s hand. Disinfectant scent intensifies the medical atmosphere. My masked face and doctor’s uniform produce an invisible pressure.

**Consent, Care, and Feminist Hapticism**

I also designed a consent form for my performative installation. The consent form marks the point at which the participant formally allows their body to be altered. Body modification needs consent because it involves touch, bodily access, technical intervention, and care. The participant’s body becomes the site of the work.

Through this project, I discovered that manicure and surgery both include a form of care, but care is not always good for the person being cared for. Care can be intimate, supportive, and protective, but it can also be disciplinary, corrective, and controlling (Puig de la Bellacasa 2017). Both manicure and surgery involve the temporary surrender of the body to another person’s expertise. Both can be performed in the name of improvement. Both can transform the body through touch. Therefore, care should not be treated as automatically ethical. Care should require consent.

This is where Foucault’s concept of biopolitics becomes useful. Foucault argues that political power not only operates by forbidding or punishing bodies, but by regulating, organising, optimising, and managing life (Foucault 2008). In a similar vein, manicure can also be understood as a small-scale form of bodily regulation. It shapes how the hand should appear, how the body should be maintained, and how femininity, cleanliness, beauty, and self-care are performed. Manicure seems soft and decorative, but it also participates in the regulation of bodily norms.

Therefore, manicure should require consent not only because it touches the body, but because it participates in the social and technical regulation of the body. The question is not whether manicure is as serious as surgery. The question is why some forms of bodily modification are recognised as requiring consent, while other feminised and cosmetic forms of modification are treated as ordinary service.

Yet the work also questions whether consent can ever be reduced to a single signature. The participant may agree before the procedure begins, but their feelings may shift as the nail is attached, as the motor vibrates, as their hand is held, or as they become aware of being observed. Consent becomes an ongoing bodily negotiation rather than a fixed administrative moment.

This is why feminist hapticism becomes necessary. I use the term feminist hapticism to name a sceptical framework for analysing how touch is organised, against patriarchal visual hegemony or ocularcentrism. Touch is never neutral. It is shaped by gendered labour, social roles, institutional protocols, material arrangements, and the unequal distribution of authority. Touch can care, but it can also discipline at the same time. It can create intimacy, and it can also expose vulnerability.

The reversibility of touch is central to this framework. To touch is always also to be touched (Merleau-Ponty 1968). This reversibility challenges the stable boundary between subject and object, active and passive, practitioner and recipient. In _Onycho Lab_, the participant touches the world through the artificial nail, but is also touched by the device, by the performer, by the clinical atmosphere, and by the institutional fiction of the procedure. The work does not treat tactility as automatically ethical or liberating. Instead, it asks who has the right to touch the body, who authorises its transformation, and under what conditions modification becomes acceptable.

Well, I began this project from the strange feeling of wearing long artificial nails. Through _Onycho Lab_, however, this intimate bodily experience unfolded into a broader inquiry. When a machine touches me, is it still only a device, or does it enter the same ethical space as human touch? Who decides what kind of care my body needs, what kind of consent is enough, and when that consent should be renewed? Does machine haptics require similar consent as human touch? Perhaps computational haptics asks us to reconsider the ethics through which touch itself is understood.

These questions inevitably return to my own role as the designer. If I decide where the sensor is placed, where and how the motor touches the skin and what kind of bodily effect the system produces, does the machine’s contact with the participant also become an indirect form of my own touch? Does computational haptics therefore redistribute power through technical design, making the designer the one who choreographs how another body is touched, sensed, and affected? _Onycho Lab_ remains unresolved at this point, leaving this question open as the next stage of the journey.
